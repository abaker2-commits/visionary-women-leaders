01 / The premise

Vision begins as a conviction that a better world is possible, then becomes the work of helping others see and build it.

Mathews describes visionary leadership as creating a clear image of a better future and sustaining commitment to it before others can fully recognize its possibility. Gloria Steinem and Jane Goodall each transformed a deeply held conviction into public language, collective action, and enduring institutions. Their work offers two distinct, yet complementary, examples of leadership with global consequences (Mathews, 2016).

Early movement leadership. Photo via Ms. magazine.

02 / Visionary leadership

Gloria Steinem

Making equality a global question

Visionary leadership

Gloria Steinem exemplified the visionary leader expansively described by Mathews. Across decades of writing, organizing, and public advocacy, she held a clear picture of a more equitable world and asked others to confront realities that had long been minimized or ignored.

At the United Nations in 2016, Steinem connected violence against women to peace, security, and democracy. She argued that a society's treatment of women offers a direct measure of how democratic and peaceful it is. Her message shifted the issue beyond a private or narrowly defined concern and framed it as a global responsibility (UN News, 2026).

“The story of women’s struggle for equality belongs to no single activist nor to any one organization but to the collective efforts of all who care about human rights.”Gloria Steinem, as quoted by the United Nations

Her work demonstrates visionary leadership through a consistent ability to name a future worth pursuing, communicate it with moral clarity, and bring others into a movement larger than any one individual.

03 / Three stages of vision

Dr. Jane Goodall

A life that enabled others to act

Visionary leadership

Jane Goodall's career maps closely to Mathews's three stages of visionary leadership: envisioning, articulating, and enabling. As a young researcher, she formed a conviction that chimpanzees had complex emotional and social lives, a belief that challenged established scientific assumptions.

01

Envisioning

She saw a more understanding of animals, ecosystems, and human responsibility.

02

Articulating

Through books, lectures, public appearances, and advocacy, she made that vision accessible to a global public.

03

Enabling

She founded the Jane Goodall Institute in 1977 and created Roots & Shoots in 1991, allowing others to carry the work forward.

Goodall died on October 1, 2025, at age 91. Her institutional legacy continues through the Jane Goodall Institute and Roots & Shoots, a youth program that has grown to more than 100 countries. This is the enabling stage at its strongest: a leader creates structures through which others can sustain the vision independently (Jane Goodall Institute, 2025).

“My job now is to try and help people understand every one of us makes a difference. And cumulatively, wise choices in how we act each day can begin to change the world.”Dr. Jane Goodall, Jane Goodall Institute

04 / Cultural intelligence

Learning to recognize the lens I bring into a room.

Cultural intelligence, or CQ, is the capability to function effectively across different cultural settings. Mathews describes four dimensions: metacognitive CQ, awareness of cultural differences as they emerge; cognitive CQ, knowledge of cultural norms and history; motivational CQ, the desire to engage across cultural lines; and behavioral CQ, the capacity to adapt words and actions appropriately (Mathews, 2016).

“Curiosity is not certainty. It is a willingness to learn what another person knows from their own life.”

A personal reflection

My husband is fourteen years younger than I am and was raised in rural Kentucky, Tennessee, and Virginia. I was raised in Maryland, served in the Army, traveled internationally, and later pursued higher education. We love one another deeply, but I have learned that shared affection does not automatically mean shared references or assumptions.

For example, I once used the phrase “someone is walking over your grave” to explain a sudden shiver. It was familiar where I grew up, but he had never heard it. Experiences like this remind me that cultural difference can appear inside a household, not only across national borders.

My current gap is primarily cognitive CQ. I recognize differences and want to close the gap, but I do not always have deeper knowledge of the cultural context that explains why something lands differently. Improving that knowledge requires listening, asking, and avoiding the assumption that my own frame is universal.

05 / Responsible leadership

Leadership is measured in the conditions it creates for others.

MACRO

External relationships

Responsible leadership shapes how an organization relates to stakeholders, communities, and society.

MESO

Organizational culture

It strengthens ethical culture, collaboration, legitimacy, and a meaningful commitment to corporate social responsibility.

MICRO

Individual followers

It supports employee motivation, job satisfaction, commitment, and organizational citizenship behavior.

Three propositions for my future practice

Proposition 2: Responsible leadership has a positive effect on building trustful stakeholder relationships. Trust must be the foundation of any team I lead.

Proposition 5: Responsible leadership can positively affect the perceived importance of corporate social responsibility. This matters especially in AI for business, where ethical responsibility cannot be a side conversation.

Proposition 8: Responsible leadership positively affects followers’ attitudes and cognitions, including job satisfaction, motivation, commitment, and citizenship behavior. I know the difference between being treated as invisible and being made to feel that my work matters (Voegtlin et al., 2012).

Voegtlin and colleagues argue that globalization can create governance gaps when public interests are insufficiently served by national regulation alone. Corporate social responsibility helps organizations sustain legitimacy with the stakeholders and communities on whom they depend (Voegtlin et al., 2012).

06 / Leadership in practice

Service, honesty, and the work of helping others grow.

An emerging servant leader with authentic leadership qualities

My emerging leadership approach is primarily servant leadership, grounded in a commitment to support, develop, and respect the people I lead. It also reflects authentic leadership through self-awareness, transparency about my present limits, and an effort to act consistently with my values.

I am currently serving as acting president of a Student Veterans Association chapter while attending two schools. Between those commitments, peer mentoring, and my business, Mildred and Rose, I have to be honest about how much information and responsibility I can process at once. I rely on structured action lists so that important commitments do not disappear under immediate demands.

Mathews places emotion at the center of leadership. Emotional intelligence involves perceiving emotions, using them to support problem solving, understanding how they change and interact, and managing them strategically to support positive outcomes. These skills matter for sustaining confidence and helping a group build a lasting sense of identity (Mathews, 2016).

07 / Continuing the work

Vision matters most when it becomes an invitation for others to lead.

Steinem and Goodall each showed that leadership is not merely influence. It is the discipline of seeing a more just, compassionate future, communicating it clearly, and creating conditions in which other people can make it real.
